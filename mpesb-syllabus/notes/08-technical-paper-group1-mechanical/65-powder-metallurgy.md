# Powder Metallurgy — Basics (पाउडर मेटलर्जी)

## Definition

A manufacturing process that produces components from **metal powders** by compacting them into a shape and then **sintering** (heat-bonding) — used when the desired metal cannot easily be cast or machined economically, or when a controlled porous structure is needed.

## Process Steps (sequence is a common MCQ)

1. **Powder production** — via atomization (molten metal sprayed into fine droplets that solidify), reduction of metal oxides, or electrolysis
2. **Blending/Mixing** — powders of different metals/sizes mixed with lubricants (to ease ejection) and binders
3. **Compaction** — powder pressed in a **die** under high pressure to form a "green compact" (fragile, not yet strong)
4. **Sintering** — green compact heated in a controlled-atmosphere furnace to a temperature **below the melting point** of the base metal — this bonds particles together by diffusion, giving the part its final strength

(Optional finishing steps: sizing/coining, impregnation, heat treatment, machining of fine features)

## Why Use Powder Metallurgy — Key Advantages

- **Near-net-shape** production — little to no material wasted (unlike machining, which removes material as chips)
- Enables working with **very high melting-point metals** (e.g., tungsten, molybdenum) that are difficult to cast or machine conventionally
- Produces **controlled porosity** parts — impossible with casting/forging — used for self-lubricating bearings (pores impregnated with oil)
- Good for **large-volume production** of small, precise components at low unit cost once tooling is made
- Can combine metals that don't alloy easily by normal melting (e.g., tungsten carbide with cobalt binder)

## Key Applications (high-yield for MCQs)

- **Self-lubricating (oil-impregnated) bearings/bushings** — the classic, most-cited PM application; porosity retains oil which seeps out under running conditions
- **Cutting tool inserts** — tungsten carbide (WC) tips bonded with cobalt, made via PM since WC's melting point is too high for conventional casting
- Small precision gears, cams, and machine parts in mass-produced items (automobiles, appliances)

## Limitations

- **Tooling (dies) cost is high** — economical only for large production runs
- **Size limitation** — large parts are difficult to compact uniformly
- **Porosity reduces mechanical strength/ductility** compared to a fully-dense wrought or cast part of the same material (unless density is deliberately increased via re-pressing/infiltration)

## Most Important Facts to Remember

- **Process sequence: powder production → blending → compaction (in a die) → sintering (below melting point).**
- **Sintering temperature is always below the metal's melting point** — bonding happens by solid-state diffusion, not melting.
- **Classic application: self-lubricating (oil-impregnated) bearings** — a fact almost guaranteed to appear as an MCQ option.
- PM is chosen specifically for **high-melting-point metals** (tungsten) and **controlled-porosity** parts — two things casting/machining cannot easily achieve.
- Economical mainly for **high-volume, small/precision parts** due to high tooling cost.

## Practice Questions

1. What is the term for the fragile, unsintered part produced immediately after compaction? → **Green compact**
2. Why is sintering carried out below the melting point rather than at/above it? → **To bond particles by solid-state diffusion while retaining the pressed shape** (melting would destroy the compacted form)
3. Name the classic powder-metallurgy application that exploits controlled porosity. → **Self-lubricating (oil-impregnated) bearings**
4. Why are tungsten carbide cutting tool tips made by powder metallurgy rather than casting? → **Tungsten's very high melting point makes conventional casting impractical**
