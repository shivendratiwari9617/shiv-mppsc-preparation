# Types of Welding (वेल्डिंग के प्रकार)

## Welding — Classification Overview

Welding processes are broadly classified based on the source and application of heat/pressure used to fuse the joint:

| Category | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Fusion Welding** | Joint is formed by **melting** the base metal (with or without filler metal) at the joint, then allowing it to solidify — no external pressure applied |
| **Pressure (Solid-State) Welding** | Joint is formed by applying **heat and pressure** together (metal may not fully melt), or pressure alone in some cases |

## Arc Welding Processes (fusion welding, using an electric arc as the heat source)

| Process | Full Form | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **SMAW** | Shielded Metal Arc Welding (also called "Stick" welding, Manual Metal Arc/MMA) | A **consumable, flux-coated electrode** is manually manipulated; the flux coating melts to form a **protective gas shield and slag layer** over the weld, shielding it from atmospheric contamination — simple, portable, widely used for general fabrication/repair, but slower and more operator-skill-dependent |
| **MIG/GMAW** | Metal Inert Gas / Gas Metal Arc Welding | A **continuously fed, consumable wire electrode**, shielded by an externally supplied **inert or semi-inert gas** (e.g., Argon, CO₂, or mixtures) — faster, easier to automate, widely used in industrial/production welding |
| **TIG/GTAW** | Tungsten Inert Gas / Gas Tungsten Arc Welding | Uses a **non-consumable tungsten electrode**, with a separate filler rod added manually (if needed) and inert gas (typically Argon) shielding — gives very high-quality, precise, clean welds, but slower and requires more operator skill; commonly used for thin sheets, precision work, and non-ferrous metals (aluminium, stainless steel) |
| **SAW** | Submerged Arc Welding | The arc is **submerged** under a blanket of granular flux (rather than a gas shield) — very high deposition rate, used for thick-section, long, straight/circular welds (e.g., pressure vessels, pipes) in automated/mechanized settings |
| **Plasma Arc Welding (PAW)** | — | Similar to TIG but the arc is constricted through a small nozzle, creating a very high-temperature, concentrated plasma jet — used for precision, high-quality welds |

## Gas Welding

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| **Oxy-Acetylene (Gas) Welding** | Heat generated by burning a mixture of **acetylene and oxygen** gas at a torch tip — versatile (can also be used for cutting, brazing, heating), portable (no electricity needed), but slower than arc welding and produces a larger heat-affected zone |

## Resistance Welding (heat generated by electrical resistance at the joint, WITH pressure — a solid-state/pressure process)

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| **Spot Welding** | Two overlapping metal sheets are held between two **electrode tips** under pressure, and a high current is passed briefly through them — localized heating fuses a small "spot," widely used in sheet-metal fabrication (e.g., automobile body panels) |
| **Seam Welding** | Similar to spot welding, but uses **rotating wheel electrodes** to produce a continuous, overlapping series of spot welds — creates a continuous, leak-tight seam |
| **Projection Welding** | Similar to spot welding, but current is concentrated at pre-formed **projections/dimples** on one of the workpieces, which act as the initial contact points |
| **Butt Welding** | Two pieces are joined **end-to-end**, heated by resistance, and pressed together |

## Solid-State (Non-Fusion) Welding Processes

| Process | Description |
|---|---|
| **Friction Welding** | Heat is generated by **friction** between two rotating/rubbing surfaces (one part typically rotates against the other under axial pressure), then pressure is applied to forge the joint — no melting, no filler metal needed |
| **Ultrasonic Welding** | High-frequency vibrations, combined with pressure, generate localized friction/heat at the joint — commonly used for thin sheets, plastics, and dissimilar/thin metal joining |
| **Explosive Welding** | A controlled explosive charge drives two metal plates together at very high velocity, creating a metallurgical bond through the impact — used for cladding dissimilar metals |
| **Forge Welding** | The oldest welding method — metal is heated (in a forge) and then hammered together while still hot/plastic (not molten) |

## Brazing & Soldering (related but distinct joining processes — commonly confused with welding)

| Process | Filler metal melting temperature | Base metal melted? |
|---|---|---|
| **Welding** | Base metal itself is melted (with or without filler) | **Yes** |
| **Brazing** | Filler metal melts **above 450°C**, but below the base metal's melting point | **No** — base metal is not melted, only the filler flows into the joint by capillary action |
| **Soldering** | Filler metal (solder) melts **below 450°C** | **No** |

**Key distinguishing fact: in both brazing and soldering, the base metal is never melted — only the filler metal melts and flows into the joint gap via capillary action; the 450°C threshold is what distinguishes brazing (above) from soldering (below).**

## Welding Defects (commonly tested — brief)

| Defect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Porosity** | Trapped gas bubbles in the weld metal |
| **Incomplete Penetration** | Weld metal does not fully penetrate through the joint thickness |
| **Undercut** | A groove melted into the base metal at the toe of the weld, left unfilled — weakens the joint |
| **Slag Inclusion** | Non-metallic slag (from flux) trapped within the weld metal |
| **Distortion/Warping** | Uneven heating/cooling causes the welded parts to warp out of shape |

## Most Important Facts to Remember

- **SMAW (Stick)**: flux-coated consumable electrode, simple/portable. **MIG (GMAW)**: continuous consumable wire, gas-shielded, fast/automatable. **TIG (GTAW)**: non-consumable tungsten electrode, gas-shielded, highest quality/precision, slower.
- **Resistance welding (spot, seam, projection) uses heat + pressure**, primarily for sheet-metal joining — a solid-state/pressure process category, distinct from arc/gas fusion welding.
- **Brazing vs Soldering**: distinguished purely by filler-metal melting temperature — **above 450°C = brazing, below 450°C = soldering** — in BOTH cases, the base metal itself is never melted (unlike welding).
- **Friction welding** joins metal via frictional heat + forging pressure, with **no melting and no filler metal**.
- **Undercut** and **incomplete penetration** are both weld-strength-reducing defects, frequently tested by name.

## Practice Questions

1. Which arc welding process uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode, giving very high weld quality but requiring more operator skill? → **TIG (GTAW)**
2. What temperature threshold distinguishes brazing from soldering? → **450°C (above = brazing, below = soldering)**
3. In brazing and soldering, is the base metal melted? → **No — only the filler metal melts and flows into the joint by capillary action**
4. Which resistance welding process is most commonly used to join automobile body sheet-metal panels? → **Spot welding**
