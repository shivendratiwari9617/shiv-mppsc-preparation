# Design of Welded & Riveted Joints (वेल्डेड व रिवेटेड जोड़ों का डिजाइन)

## Riveted Joints

### Riveted Joint — Basic Concept
Two (or more) plates are joined permanently by inserting a **rivet** (a soft metal pin with a preformed head) through matching holes and forming a second head on the protruding end (by hammering/pressing) — creates a permanent mechanical joint.

### Types of Riveted Joints (by plate arrangement)

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Lap Joint** | Plates **overlap** each other, rivets pass through the overlapping region |
| **Butt Joint** | Plates are placed **edge-to-edge**, joined using one or two additional **cover/strap plates** on top of (and/or below) the joint |

### Types of Riveted Joints (by rivet arrangement/rows)
**Single-riveted, Double-riveted, Triple-riveted** (referring to the number of rows of rivets); and, by pattern, **Chain riveting** (rivets in successive rows aligned directly behind each other) vs **Zig-zag (Staggered) riveting** (rivets in alternate rows offset from each other, staggered pattern) — staggered riveting generally gives a **higher joint efficiency** than chain riveting for the same pitch/rivet size, since it creates a less direct failure path across the plate.

### Failure Modes of a Riveted Joint (must check all, for design)

| Failure Mode | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tearing of the plate** (between rivet holes, or at the edge) | Plate fails in tension across the weakened section (net area, after subtracting rivet hole area) |
| **Shearing of the rivet** | Rivet shaft fails in shear, at the plate interface(s) |
| **Crushing (Bearing failure) of the rivet or plate** | Rivet or plate material crushes/deforms due to bearing (contact) pressure between rivet and plate hole |
| **Tearing of the plate at the edge** | Insufficient margin (edge distance) between the rivet hole and plate edge causes the plate to tear out |

**Design principle: the joint must be checked against ALL applicable failure modes, and the strength is governed by the WEAKEST (lowest-capacity) mode.**

### Efficiency of a Riveted Joint

**η = (Strength of the riveted joint, i.e., the LOWEST of the failure-mode strengths) / (Strength of the solid, un-riveted plate)**

A riveted joint is always **weaker** than the solid plate it joins (η < 100%), because rivet holes remove material and create stress concentration — this efficiency concept directly links to point 9 (thin cylinder joint efficiency).

## Welded Joints

### Welding — Basic Concept
Joining two metal pieces **permanently** by applying heat (with or without pressure, with or without filler material) to fuse them together at the joint — unlike riveting, welding does not require removing material (no holes), giving generally higher joint efficiency.

### Types of Welded Joints (by joint geometry)

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Butt Weld** | Plates joined edge-to-edge, in the same plane |
| **Fillet Weld** | Plates joined at an angle (typically 90°, lap or T-configuration), weld metal fills the triangular groove at the junction — the most common type in structural/machine fabrication |
| **Lap Weld** | Overlapping plates joined along the edge(s) of the overlap, using fillet welds |

### Fillet Weld — Design Basics
The critical/weakest section of a fillet weld is its **throat** — the minimum cross-sectional dimension through the weld, at 45° to the leg (assuming an equal-leg fillet):

**Throat thickness (t) = 0.707 × Leg/Size (h) of the weld** (i.e., h × sin45°)

Strength is typically calculated based on this throat area resisting shear.

### Welding Processes (brief overview)
- **Arc Welding**: heat generated by an electric arc between electrode and workpiece (most common industrial process; includes SMAW/"stick" welding, MIG, TIG — see also point 43).
- **Gas Welding**: heat from burning a fuel gas (typically oxy-acetylene) with oxygen.
- **Resistance Welding**: heat generated by electrical resistance at the joint (e.g., spot welding).

## Riveted vs Welded Joints — Comparison (very high-yield)

| | Riveted Joint | Welded Joint |
|---|---|---|
| Material removal | Yes — holes drilled/punched (weakens plate) | No — no material removed |
| Joint efficiency | Lower (typically) | Higher (typically) |
| Weight | Heavier (extra rivets, overlap material) | Lighter (more material-efficient) |
| Skill/equipment | Comparatively simpler tools | Requires skilled welders, specialized equipment |
| Vibration/fatigue resistance | Generally better under **vibration/shock loading**, since rivets have some inherent flexibility (can accommodate small movements) | More susceptible to fatigue failure at the weld, and can develop **residual stresses**/distortion from the heat of welding |
| Inspection/quality control | Easier to visually inspect | Requires specialized NDT (non-destructive testing) for internal flaw detection |
| Disassembly | Can (with effort) be disassembled by removing rivets | Essentially **permanent** — cannot be disassembled without destroying the joint |
| Modern usage | Largely replaced by welding in most structural/machine applications, but still used where vibration resistance matters (e.g., aircraft structures, boiler shells historically) | Dominant modern joining method for most structural and machine fabrication |

## Most Important Facts to Remember

- Riveted joint failure modes to check: **tearing of plate, shearing of rivet, crushing/bearing of rivet or plate hole** — design strength = the **weakest** of these.
- **Zig-zag (staggered) riveting gives higher efficiency than chain riveting** for the same pitch.
- **Joint efficiency = Joint strength / Solid plate strength** — always < 100% for a riveted joint (due to rivet holes).
- Fillet weld throat thickness = **0.707 × weld leg size (h)** — the standard 45°-throat formula.
- **Riveted joints tolerate vibration/shock better** (some inherent flexibility); **welded joints are generally lighter and give higher efficiency**, but can suffer residual stress and are essentially permanent/non-disassemblable.

## Practice Questions

1. In a riveted joint, what are the three main failure modes that must be checked in design? → **Tearing of the plate, shearing of the rivet, and crushing/bearing failure**
2. Which riveting pattern generally gives higher joint efficiency — chain riveting or zig-zag (staggered) riveting? → **Zig-zag (staggered) riveting**
3. What is the formula for the throat thickness of an equal-leg fillet weld, in terms of the leg size (h)? → **t = 0.707h**
4. Why do riveted joints sometimes remain preferred over welded joints in structures subject to significant vibration or shock loading? → **Rivets have some inherent flexibility that helps absorb/accommodate vibration and shock, whereas welded joints (and the heat-affected zone) can be more prone to fatigue cracking under such loading**
