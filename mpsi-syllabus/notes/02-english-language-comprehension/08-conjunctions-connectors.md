# Conjunctions and Connectors

**Subject:** English Language Comprehension | **Section:** Grammar Fundamentals | **Exam relevance:** MPSI along with MPPSC, Vyapam/ESB, Patwari, MP Police — conjunction-pair redundancy errors ("although...but") are among the most repeated error-spotting questions.

---

## 1. Recap: What is a Conjunction?

A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses (introduced briefly in point 1). This note expands each type in depth.

---

## 2. Coordinating Conjunctions — FANBOYS

Join two **independent (equal-status) clauses or elements**.

| Conjunction | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **F**or | reason | He didn't go, for he was ill. |
| **A**nd | addition | She sings and dances. |
| **N**or | negative addition | He neither smokes nor drinks. |
| **B**ut | contrast | He is poor but honest. |
| **O**r | alternative | You can walk or take the bus. |
| **Y**et | contrast (despite) | It was raining, yet they went out. |
| **S**o | result | He was tired, so he went to sleep. |

---

## 3. Subordinating Conjunctions

Join a **dependent (subordinate) clause** to an independent clause.

| Function | Conjunctions |
|---|---|
| Cause/Reason | because, since, as |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that |
| Contrast/Concession | although, though, even though, whereas, while |
| Time | when, while, before, after, until, as soon as |
| Purpose | so that, in order that |
| Result | so...that, such...that |

Examples: "He failed **because** he didn't study." / "**Although** it was raining, we went out."

---

## 4. Correlative Conjunctions — Work in Pairs

| Pair | Example |
|---|---|
| either...or | Either you or I am wrong. |
| neither...nor | Neither he nor his friends were present. |
| both...and | Both Ram and Shyam came. |
| not only...but also | Not only did he apologize, but he also compensated us. |
| whether...or | I don't know whether he will come or not. |

**Note:** "either...or" and "neither...nor" agreement follows Rule 3 from point 3 (Subject-Verb Agreement) — the verb agrees with the **nearer** subject.

---

## 5. Connectors/Linking Words (Transition Words)

Used in comprehension passages and descriptive writing to link ideas across sentences.

| Function | Examples |
|---|---|
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides, also |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast, whereas |
| Cause-effect | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence |
| Sequence | first, then, next, finally, subsequently |
| Example | for instance, for example, such as |
| Summary | in conclusion, to sum up, in short |

---

## 6. Common Errors — Redundant Conjunction Pairs (Very High-Yield)

Hindi speakers commonly carry over the habit of pairing two conjunctions (as in "यद्यपि...परंतु") into English, where this is **grammatically wrong** — English uses **only one** conjunction per relationship.

| Wrong | Correct |
|---|---|
| Although he is rich, **but** he is unhappy. | Although he is rich, he is unhappy. |
| Because he was late, **so** he missed the train. | Because he was late, he missed the train. |
| As he was ill, **so** he could not come. | As he was ill, he could not come. |

### Other common errors
- **"Not only...but also"** requires parallel structure, and inversion (auxiliary before subject) when it starts the sentence: "**Not only did** he sing, but he **also** danced."
- **"Neither...nor"** must be followed by the **same grammatical form** on both sides (parallelism): "He neither **smokes** nor **drinks**." (not "neither smokes nor drinking")

---

## 7. Practice — High-Probability Self-Test

| Sentence | Correction needed? |
|---|---|
| "Though he is poor, but he is honest." | Yes — remove "but" |
| "He is neither rich nor famous." | Correct as is |
| "Not only she sings well but also dances well." | Needs inversion: "Not only **does** she sing well but also dance well." |
| "Because it rained, so the match was cancelled." | Yes — remove "so" |

---

## 8. Most Important Exam-Focused Points

1. The **"although...but" / "because...so" redundant pair error (Section 6)** is the single most repeated conjunction question in state-level English papers — check for it first in any error-spotting sentence with "although," "though," "because," or "as."
2. Learn **coordinating vs subordinating vs correlative** conjunctions as three distinct groups (Sections 2-4) — questions often ask you to classify which type a given conjunction is.
3. **Correlative conjunctions require parallel structure** — mismatched verb forms across the pair are a common trap.
4. Connectors (Section 5) matter directly for point 19 (Reading comprehension) and point 20 (Cloze test), where choosing the right transition word for logical flow is exactly what's being tested.
