# Sentence Rearrangement / Para Jumbles

**Subject:** English Language Comprehension | **Section:** Comprehension (Skill-Based) | **Exam relevance:** MPSI along with MPPSC, Vyapam/ESB, Patwari, MP Police — para jumbles test logical/structural understanding of English paragraphs rather than grammar rules directly.

---

## 1. What is Sentence Rearrangement?

Several sentences (usually 4-6), labeled A/B/C/D/E, are given in **jumbled/random order**. You must determine the sequence that forms a logically coherent paragraph.

---

## 2. Strategy — Step by Step

1. **Find the opening sentence** — it introduces the topic in general terms, using a proper noun or general concept, not a pronoun referring back to something unstated.
2. **Find the closing sentence** — it often draws a conclusion or summarizes, sometimes starting with "Thus," "Therefore," "Finally," or "In conclusion."
3. **Trace pronoun references** — if a sentence contains "he/she/it/they/this/that," find the sentence that first introduces that noun; the reference must come **after** its introduction.
4. **Trace connector/transition words** — "however," "moreover," "therefore," "for example" signal how one sentence relates to the one before it.
5. **Look for chronological/logical order clues** — dates, "first...then...finally," or cause stated before its effect.
6. **Find natural "linked pairs"** first — two sentences that clearly must sit adjacent to each other (due to a pronoun reference or direct elaboration) — then arrange the pairs relative to each other, rather than trying to place all sentences independently at once.

---

## 3. Indicators a Sentence IS the Opening Sentence

- Introduces a general topic, person, place, or concept **by name**, not by pronoun.
- States a general fact, background, or definition.
- Does **not** contain "this/that/these/such/also/too" (words that refer back to something already mentioned).

## 4. Indicators a Sentence Is NOT the Opening Sentence

- Starts with a pronoun (he, she, it, they) with no prior noun reference in that sentence.
- Starts with a connector implying continuation: But, However, Also, Moreover, Therefore, So, For example.
- Starts with "This/That/These/Those" referring to something not yet introduced.

---

## 5. Worked Example

Jumbled sentences:
- **(A)** This led to a significant increase in crop production.
- **(B)** In the 1960s, India faced a severe food shortage.
- **(C)** As a result, the country introduced new farming techniques.
- **(D)** The Green Revolution, as it came to be known, transformed Indian agriculture.
- **(E)** These techniques included high-yield seeds and modern irrigation methods.

**Correct order: B → C → E → A → D**

| Step | Reasoning |
|---|---|
| B first | Introduces the topic (food shortage) by name — no pronoun reference needed, classic opening sentence |
| C next | "As a result" directly follows from B's food shortage — a logical connector linking to the immediately prior sentence |
| E next | "These techniques" refers back to "new farming techniques" first mentioned in C — must follow C directly |
| A next | "This led to..." refers to the techniques just elaborated in E |
| D last | Names/summarizes the whole phenomenon as "the Green Revolution" — a natural concluding/labeling sentence |

---

## 6. Practice — High-Probability Self-Test

Using the rules above, identify:
| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Which sentence type is most likely to open a paragraph? | The one introducing the topic by name, without a pronoun or continuation word |
| A sentence starting with "However," can it be the opening sentence? | No — "However" always signals a contrast with something stated before it |
| If sentence X says "These methods worked well," what must you find? | The sentence that first introduces "these methods" — it must come immediately before X |

---

## 7. Most Important Exam-Focused Points

1. **Never try to place all sentences at once** — find the most obvious linked pair first (usually a pronoun-reference pair), then build outward from there.
2. The **opening and closing sentences** are usually the easiest to identify first — locking those two down narrows the possibilities for the middle sentences considerably.
3. **Connector words (Section 2, point 4)** are the strongest structural clue in this topic — always check what a sentence's first word implies about its relationship to prior content.
4. This topic doesn't test grammar directly but relies on the same connector-word knowledge as point 8 (Conjunctions and connectors) and point 20 (Cloze test) — strengthening those helps here too.
