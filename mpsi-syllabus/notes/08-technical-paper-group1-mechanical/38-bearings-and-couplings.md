# Machine Elements — Bearings & Couplings (मशीन तत्व — बेयरिंग व कपलिंग)

## Bearing — Basic Function

A machine element that **supports a rotating (or sliding) shaft**, allowing relative motion between the shaft and its housing while minimizing friction and carrying the load transmitted by the shaft (radial and/or axial/thrust loads).

## Classification of Bearings — by Nature of Contact

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Sliding-Contact (Plain/Journal) Bearing** | Shaft surface slides directly against the bearing surface (typically separated by a thin lubricant film) — simple, quiet, high load capacity, but higher starting friction |
| **Rolling-Contact (Antifriction) Bearing** | Uses rolling elements (balls or rollers) between inner and outer races — much lower friction (especially at starting), but generally more limited load/speed combination and more sensitive to shock loading than plain bearings |

## Rolling-Contact Bearings — Types

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Ball Bearing** | Uses spherical balls as rolling elements — suited for **light-to-moderate loads, higher speeds** |
| **Roller Bearing** | Uses cylindrical/tapered/spherical rollers — larger contact area than balls, suited for **heavier loads** |
| **Deep Groove Ball Bearing** | Most common general-purpose type — handles both radial and moderate axial (thrust) load |
| **Angular Contact Ball Bearing** | Designed specifically to carry **combined radial and axial (thrust) load** effectively |
| **Thrust Bearing** | Designed specifically to carry **axial (thrust) load only**, with little/no radial load capacity |
| **Tapered Roller Bearing** | Rollers are conical (tapered) — handles combined radial and thrust loads, common in vehicle wheel hubs, gearboxes |

## Sliding (Journal) Bearings — Lubrication Regimes

| Regime | Description |
|---|---|
| **Thick Film (Hydrodynamic) Lubrication** | A continuous, load-supporting oil film **completely separates** the shaft and bearing surfaces, generated by the shaft's own rotation dragging oil into a converging wedge — no metal-to-metal contact once established; lowest friction/wear regime |
| **Thin Film (Boundary) Lubrication** | Lubricant film is too thin to fully separate the surfaces — some **metal-to-metal contact** occurs at surface asperities, higher friction/wear; occurs particularly at low speed, high load, or during starting/stopping |
| **Mixed-Film Lubrication** | An intermediate condition, partly hydrodynamic and partly boundary |

**Key fact: a journal bearing typically starts in boundary lubrication (at rest/low speed) and transitions to full hydrodynamic (thick-film) lubrication once sufficient speed is reached** — this startup transition is when journal bearings experience the most wear, since the protective oil wedge hasn't fully developed yet.

## Bearing Materials
Common plain-bearing (bush) materials: **babbitt (white metal), bronze, brass, gunmetal, cast iron** — selected for good compatibility (won't seize/gall against the shaft material), embeddability (can absorb small hard particles without scoring the shaft), and conformability.

## L10 Life (Rolling Bearing Life Rating)
The number of revolutions (or hours, at a given speed) that **90% of a group of identical bearings** will complete/exceed before the first evidence of fatigue (spalling) failure appears — the standard statistical bearing-life rating used in bearing selection/catalogues.

## Coupling — Basic Function

A device used to **permanently (or semi-permanently) connect two shafts** end-to-end (in-line), so they rotate together and transmit torque as a single effective unit — unlike a clutch (point 37), a coupling does not provide a means of engaging/disengaging during operation.

## Types of Couplings

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rigid Coupling** | Connects two shafts **rigidly**, with no flexibility — requires precise alignment of the two shafts, since any misalignment directly induces additional stress/vibration |
| **Sleeve (Muff) Coupling** | Simplest rigid type — a hollow cylindrical sleeve fits over the ends of both shafts, secured by a key and/or set-screws |
| **Flange Coupling** | Rigid type — each shaft has a flange (disc) keyed to it; the two flanges are bolted together — common, robust, easy to assemble/disassemble |
| **Flexible Coupling** | Permits some degree of **misalignment** (angular, parallel/radial, or axial) between the connected shafts, and/or absorbs shock/vibration, via a flexible element (rubber/elastomer bush, flexible disc, or a mechanical linkage like gear teeth or bellows) |
| **Oldham Coupling** | A specific flexible coupling design that accommodates **parallel (radial) misalignment** between two shafts, using an intermediate floating disc with perpendicular sliding tongues/slots |
| **Universal (Hooke's) Joint** | A specific flexible coupling that accommodates significant **angular misalignment** between two intersecting shafts — the classic example is the vehicle driveshaft (connecting the gearbox to a differential/axle at a changing angle, e.g., as suspension moves) |

## Rigid vs Flexible Coupling — Comparison

| | Rigid Coupling | Flexible Coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Alignment requirement | Requires **precise** shaft alignment | Tolerates some **misalignment** |
| Vibration/shock absorption | None (transmits directly) | Absorbs some shock/vibration |
| Typical use | Shafts that are already well-aligned, e.g., within the same rigid machine frame | Connecting shafts that may shift/misalign in operation (e.g., motor-to-pump coupling on separate bases, vehicle driveshafts) |

## Most Important Facts to Remember

- **Rolling-contact bearings** = lower friction, especially at starting (balls/rollers); **Sliding (journal) bearings** = higher load capacity generally, but higher starting friction, rely on a **hydrodynamic oil wedge** once running.
- Journal bearings experience most wear during **starting/stopping** (boundary lubrication phase), before the full hydrodynamic film develops.
- **L10 life**: revolutions at which **90%** of a bearing batch survives without fatigue failure — the standard bearing-life rating metric.
- **Coupling** connects two shafts **permanently** (no disengagement in operation); **Clutch** allows engagement/disengagement — a frequently tested distinction from point 37.
- **Rigid coupling** (sleeve, flange) needs precise alignment; **Flexible coupling** (Oldham for parallel misalignment, Universal/Hooke's joint for angular misalignment) tolerates misalignment and absorbs shock.

## Practice Questions

1. What is the key difference between a coupling and a clutch? → **A coupling permanently connects two shafts with no provision for engagement/disengagement during operation; a clutch allows the connection to be engaged and disengaged as needed**
2. During which phase of operation does a journal (sliding-contact) bearing typically experience the most wear? → **During starting/stopping, when only boundary (thin-film) lubrication is present, before the hydrodynamic oil film fully develops**
3. Which type of flexible coupling is specifically designed to accommodate significant angular misalignment between two shafts, as used in a vehicle driveshaft? → **Universal (Hooke's) joint**
4. What does "L10 life" represent for a rolling-contact bearing? → **The number of revolutions/hours at which 90% of a group of identical bearings will have completed without fatigue failure**
