# Corrosion — Types & Prevention (संक्षारण)

## Definition

The **destructive deterioration of a metal** through chemical or (more commonly) **electrochemical reaction** with its surrounding environment (moisture, oxygen, acids, salts) — metal effectively reverts toward its naturally-occurring oxidized state (e.g., iron rusting back toward iron oxide).

## Electrochemical Corrosion — Basic Mechanism (high-yield)

Requires an **anode**, a **cathode**, an **electrolyte** (moisture film), and a **metallic path** for electron flow between anode and cathode — essentially a tiny corrosion "battery" forming on the metal surface:

- **Anode**: where **oxidation** occurs — metal atoms lose electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte as ions (**this is where actual metal loss/corrosion happens**)
- **Cathode**: where **reduction** occurs (e.g., oxygen + water + electrons → hydroxide ions) — the cathode itself is **not consumed**

## Types of Corrosion (very high-yield classification)

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Uniform corrosion** | Corrodes evenly across the entire exposed surface — most common but also most predictable/manageable |
| **Pitting corrosion** | Highly **localized**, forms small deep pits/holes while surrounding surface stays largely intact — dangerous because it's hard to detect yet can cause rapid perforation |
| **Galvanic corrosion** | Occurs when **two dissimilar metals** are in electrical contact in the presence of an electrolyte — the more "active" (anodic) metal corrodes preferentially, protecting the more "noble" (cathodic) one |
| **Crevice corrosion** | Localized attack within narrow gaps/crevices (under gaskets, bolt heads, washers) where stagnant electrolyte and oxygen depletion occur |
| **Intergranular corrosion** | Attack concentrated along **grain boundaries** of the metal (e.g., in improperly heat-treated stainless steel, due to chromium carbide precipitation at grain boundaries — "sensitization") |
| **Stress corrosion cracking (SCC)** | Combined effect of **tensile stress + corrosive environment** causing crack propagation, even though neither factor alone would cause failure |

## Galvanic Series & Sacrificial Protection Principle

Metals can be ranked by their tendency to corrode (their "nobility") — when two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in an electrolyte, the **more active (less noble) metal becomes the anode and corrodes**, while the **more noble metal is protected** (acts as cathode). This principle is deliberately exploited in cathodic protection (below).

## Corrosion Prevention Methods (high-yield)

- **Protective coatings**: paint, galvanizing (zinc coating on steel), electroplating (chromium/nickel), anodizing (aluminium) — physically isolate metal from the electrolyte/atmosphere
- **Cathodic protection**: 
  - **Sacrificial anode method**: a more active/reactive metal (commonly **zinc or magnesium**) is deliberately connected to the structure to be protected (e.g., ship hulls, buried pipelines) — the sacrificial anode corrodes instead of the main structure, and is periodically replaced
  - **Impressed current method**: an external DC power source forces current through an inert anode to make the structure cathodic (used for larger/more critical structures like pipelines)
- **Use of corrosion-resistant alloys**: e.g., **stainless steel** (chromium forms a passive, self-healing oxide film on the surface)
- **Corrosion inhibitors**: chemicals added to the environment (e.g., in cooling water systems) that reduce the corrosion reaction rate
- **Design measures**: avoiding dissimilar-metal contact where possible, avoiding crevices/stagnant-water pockets, proper drainage

## Most Important Facts to Remember

- Corrosion is fundamentally an **electrochemical (oxidation-reduction) process** — needs anode, cathode, electrolyte, and a conducting path.
- **Anode = where metal is actually lost** (oxidation); cathode is not consumed.
- **Galvanic corrosion**: two dissimilar metals in contact — the more active one corrodes, protecting the nobler one — this is the exact principle used deliberately in **sacrificial anode cathodic protection**.
- **Zinc/magnesium** = classic sacrificial anode materials for protecting steel structures (ship hulls, pipelines).
- **Stainless steel resists corrosion via a passive chromium-oxide film**, not by being chemically inert.
- **Pitting** and **crevice corrosion** are both localized and dangerous precisely because they're hard to detect visually compared to uniform corrosion.

## Practice Questions

1. At which electrode (anode or cathode) does actual metal loss occur during electrochemical corrosion? → **Anode (oxidation site)**
2. What is the principle behind using a zinc block to protect a ship's steel hull? → **Sacrificial anode cathodic protection** — zinc is more active than steel, so it corrodes preferentially, protecting the hull
3. Name the type of corrosion that occurs specifically because two different metals are in electrical contact. → **Galvanic corrosion**
4. Why does stainless steel resist corrosion despite containing iron? → **A thin, self-healing passive chromium-oxide film forms on its surface, protecting the underlying metal**
